Tickets for a School Christmas Program: A Simple Plan for Teachers and Parents

By the Telar Labs team · November 25, 2026 · 5 min read

Every family wants to see their child on stage, and most school halls can’t fit everyone at once. A simple ticket plan keeps it fair, keeps the hall safe and saves teachers from a week of “Can I have two more seats?” messages.

1. Start with the hall, not the ticket

Before deciding anything else, find out:

  • How many people the hall can safely hold, according to the school or the venue. Subtract the space needed for performers, teachers, the stage crew and aisles.
  • How many students are performing, and in which grades.
  • Whether the program can run more than once. Two or three shorter showings are often better than one long, crowded one.

Divide the seats by the number of performing students. That tells you how many seats each family can fairly have.

2. Set a fair limit per family

A clear limit, announced in advance, prevents most complaints. A common approach:

  • 2 to 4 seats per performing student. Siblings who are also performing count separately.
  • Babies on a parent’s lap don’t need a ticket, but say so clearly.
  • A waiting list for extra seats, released a few days before the program if seats remain.

Asking for the student’s name and class when parents get tickets helps teachers spot duplicates and makes sure every child has someone in the audience.

3. Split the showings by grade if you need to

If the hall is small, schedule showings by grade level. For example, Kindergarten to Grade 2 at 9 AM and Grades 3 to 6 at 1 PM. Each showing gets its own seat limit. Parents of children in different groups can book both. This also keeps the program shorter for young children, who get restless quickly.

4. Free or paid?

  • Free tickets are the most common choice for school programs. Their job is fair seating, not money.
  • A small paid ticket, for example $3 to $5, can help cover costumes, decorations or a class fund. Keep it low, and make sure no family is left out because of cost. A quiet “contact the class adviser if this is difficult” line on the ticket page goes a long way.
  • A raffle or snack sale on the day is often a better fundraiser than raising the ticket price.

5. Collect money safely

Cash in envelopes passed through children’s bags is the source of most school-event headaches: lost envelopes, wrong change and no record of who paid. Better options:

  • Payment directly to the school or parents’ association account, using an e-wallet or bank QR code in the organization’s name, not a teacher’s personal account.
  • One clear record of every family’s tickets and payment, which a teacher or treasurer approves.
  • Cash at a single table, with two people, on a set day, if some families prefer to pay in person.

At the end, the treasurer should be able to show exactly how much was collected and from whom.

6. One clear message to parents

Send a single message, through the channels parents already use, such as the class group chat, the school app or a printed note. Include:

  1. The date, time and place of each showing, and which grades perform in each.
  2. The seat limit per student and how to get tickets, with one link.
  3. The deadline for getting tickets.
  4. The price, if any, and how to pay.
  5. Rules for photos and videos, and whether strollers are allowed.

Send one reminder a week before and another the day before. Answer questions by pointing back to the same message, so information doesn’t split across ten chat threads.

7. Volunteers and roles

A school program usually needs:

  • 2 to 3 people at the door to scan tickets and help families find seats
  • 1 person for the waiting list and late arrivals
  • Ushers to keep aisles clear for safety
  • Backstage helpers with each class, so teachers can focus on the children

Parents are usually happy to help when roles are clear and short. Offer 30-minute shifts so volunteers can still watch their own child.

8. Check-in at the door

  • Volunteers can scan QR tickets with their own phones. Keep a printed list of names by class as a backup.
  • Open the doors 20 to 30 minutes before each showing.
  • Between showings, give the hall 15 to 20 minutes to empty before letting the next group in. A one-way flow (in by one door, out by another) helps a lot.
  • Keep the front rows for younger siblings, grandparents and guests who need easier access, and say so in advance.

9. Photos, videos and privacy

Parents will want to record their children. Decide the rules in advance, in line with your school’s policy:

  • Ask parents to film only from their seats, so aisles stay clear and nobody blocks the view.
  • Remind families not to post other children’s faces online without permission.
  • Consider asking one volunteer to record the whole program for families, so everyone else can simply watch.

10. After the program

  • Thank families, volunteers and teachers in a short message, with a photo or two.
  • If you collected money, share the total and what it will be used for.
  • Note what to change next year: number of showings, seat limits, door flow.

Want to share the program afterwards? Our guide to making a school event recap video shows how to put one together quickly.

Quick checklist

  • Safe capacity checked, minus space for performers and aisles
  • Seat limit per student and a waiting list
  • Showings split by grade if needed, each with its own limit
  • Free or low-cost tickets, with a quiet option for families who need help
  • Payments to the school or parents’ association account, with one clear record
  • One message to parents with one link and a deadline
  • Door volunteers, phone scanning and a printed backup list
  • Photo and video rules agreed in advance
