How to Sell Tickets for a Church Christmas Concert

By the Telar Labs team · November 25, 2026 · 5 min read

A church Christmas concert is one of the warmest events of the year, and also one of the busiest for the people organizing it. Tickets help you know who is coming, avoid an overflowing sanctuary and, if you charge, raise money for a cause. This guide walks through the decisions in the order you’ll face them.

1. Decide: free tickets, paid tickets, or both

Not every church concert should charge admission, but almost every one benefits from tickets. Even free tickets tell you how many people to expect, so you can plan chairs, parking, refreshments and the number of ushers.

  • Free tickets work well for outreach concerts, where the goal is to welcome neighbors who don’t usually attend church. A free ticket still creates a small commitment, and fewer seats go empty.
  • Paid tickets make sense when the concert funds something specific: a mission trip, a building fund, a new sound system or a local charity. Say so on the ticket page. People give more happily when they know where the money goes.
  • Both is common: free tickets for church members and children, paid tickets for a reserved section or a second, larger performance.

Whatever you choose, agree on it with your pastor and finance committee early, so nobody is surprised.

2. Set ticket types and prices

Keep the choices few and clear. A typical setup looks like this:

  • Adult: $10
  • Child (under 12): free or $3
  • Family ticket (2 adults + up to 3 children): $25
  • Supporter ticket: $25 or more, for people who want to give extra

Prices depend on your community. Look at what a family would spend on a night out nearby and stay well below it. If you’re raising money for a cause, the supporter ticket often brings in more than you’d expect, because it gives generous people an easy way to help.

3. Work out how many seats you really have

Count usable seats, not total seats. Pews near the choir, behind pillars or blocked by cameras and sound equipment don’t count. Then hold some back:

  • Reserve a few rows for performers’ families, older members and guests with mobility needs.
  • Keep about 5% free for latecomers and walk-ins on the night.
  • If demand is high, add a second performance (for example, a Saturday evening and a Sunday afternoon) instead of squeezing everyone in. Give each one its own ticket limit.

When tickets sell out, stop sales automatically. Overselling a sanctuary is the fastest way to turn a joyful evening into a stressful one.

4. A simple timeline

  1. 6 weeks before: confirm the date, the program and rehearsals. Decide ticket types, prices and seat limits.
  2. 5 weeks before: open ticket sales. Announce it after every service and in the church bulletin.
  3. 3 to 4 weeks before: share a short video or poster on social media and in church group chats. Ask choir members to invite their own families and friends; personal invitations sell the most tickets.
  4. 1 week before: send a reminder to everyone who has a ticket, with the time, the address, parking tips and how check-in works.
  5. Day before: close online sales if you want an exact count, or leave them open until the start time if you’ll also welcome walk-ins.

5. Handle the money transparently

Church money deserves extra care, because trust is the most valuable thing your congregation has. A few habits make a big difference:

  • Have payments go straight to the church’s account, not to a volunteer’s personal account. E-wallet and bank QR codes in the church’s name make this easy.
  • Two people, always: one volunteer approves payments and another checks the totals. For cash at the door, two people count it together and both sign the count sheet.
  • Match every ticket to a payment. A ticketing page that lists each order with its payment status saves hours of matching screenshots in a group chat.
  • Report the result to the congregation afterwards: tickets sold, money raised, costs and what the money will be used for.

6. A note on music licensing

Many Christmas songs are still under copyright, and some church licenses that cover worship services don’t cover ticketed concerts. Before you sell tickets, check with your church office or your music licensing provider whether your concert is covered. Traditional carols in the public domain, like Silent Night and O Come, All Ye Faithful, are usually safe, but specific arrangements can still be protected.

7. Check-in at the door

The first minutes at the door set the mood for the whole evening. Plan them like this:

  • Two lines: one for people with tickets on their phones, one for cash buyers and anyone who needs help.
  • Volunteers with phones: ushers can scan QR tickets with their own phones, so no special equipment is needed. Give each one a short practice run before the doors open.
  • A printed list as backup, in case of a weak signal or a dead phone battery. Look people up by name.
  • Open the doors 30 to 45 minutes early. Church audiences like to arrive early and greet each other.
  • Have a few volunteers ready to help older guests and families with small children to their seats.

8. On the night

  • Start on time. Families with young children will thank you.
  • If you take a special offering during the concert, say what it’s for, and keep it separate from ticket money in your records.
  • Ask one volunteer to take photos and short clips for a thank-you post and next year’s promotion. Respect anyone who doesn’t want to be filmed, especially children.

9. After the concert

  • Send a thank-you message to ticket holders within two days, with a photo or short video and what the concert raised.
  • Thank the choir, musicians and volunteers by name in the next bulletin or service.
  • Write down what worked and what didn’t: start time, number of ushers, sell-out date, line length at the door. Next year’s team will be grateful.

Quick checklist

  • Free, paid or both, agreed with church leadership
  • Ticket types and prices set, with a clear purpose for the money
  • Usable seats counted, rows reserved, second performance if needed
  • Sales open 5 weeks before, with announcements and personal invitations
  • Payments going to the church’s own account, with two people checking
  • Music licensing checked for a ticketed event
  • Door plan: two lines, phone scanning, printed backup list
  • Thank-you message and report afterwards
