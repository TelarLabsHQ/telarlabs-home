How to Sell Event Tickets When Buyers Pay You Directly

By the Telar Labs team · November 20, 2026 · Updated November 22, 2026 · 4 min read

For small events, the simplest way to collect ticket money is often the oldest: buyers pay you, directly, using the e-wallet or bank account you already have. The hard part isn't the payment. It's keeping track of who paid.

Why organizers choose direct payment

Big ticketing platforms collect the money for you, then pay you later, minus their fees. That's convenient for large events, but for a school play, a church concert or a community fun run, it has drawbacks:

  • Fees. A percentage plus a fixed amount per ticket adds up, especially on cheap tickets.
  • Waiting. You may not receive the money until after the event, when you've already paid for the venue and food.
  • Payment methods. Your buyers may prefer a local e-wallet, a bank QR code or cash, which international platforms may not support.

With direct payment, the money arrives in your own account immediately, in the way your buyers already pay each other every day.

Step 1: Choose your payment methods

Offer two or three methods your buyers actually use. Too many options confuse people; too few lose sales.

  • E-wallets like GCash, Maya, GoPay, DANA, PayNow, PromptPay, Touch 'n Go, WeChat Pay, Alipay or MoMo, depending on your country.
  • Bank transfer, with your account name and number, or a bank QR code.
  • A card payment link from a payment service, for buyers who prefer cards.
  • Cash, paid at the door or to a class representative.

Use an account in the organization's name if you have one. If you use a personal account, tell buyers clearly whose name will appear, so they know they're paying the right person.

Step 2: Ask for proof and a reference number

When buyers send money directly, you need to match each payment to an order. Ask every buyer to send:

  • a screenshot of the payment confirmation, and
  • the reference or transaction number.

Then check each one against your e-wallet or bank history before confirming the ticket. Screenshots can be edited; the reference number in your own account history can't. This one habit prevents most payment problems.

Step 3: Set a time limit to pay

Without a deadline, people reserve seats "for later" and some never pay. Set a pay-by time, such as 24 or 48 hours after reserving. If no proof arrives in time, the seat goes back on sale. Tell buyers about the deadline clearly when they reserve.

Step 4: Give every buyer a real ticket

Once you confirm a payment, send the buyer a ticket they can show at the door. A ticket with a unique QR code or code number is better than a name on a list, because:

  • it can be checked quickly with a phone,
  • it can be used only once, so a forwarded screenshot won't get two people in, and
  • the buyer has something to keep, which makes the event feel official.

Doing it with a spreadsheet vs. a ticket page

You can run all of this with a group chat, a spreadsheet and a lot of patience. For 20 tickets, that's manageable. For 200, it becomes a part-time job of scrolling through screenshots, updating rows and answering "Did you receive my payment?"

A ticket page built for direct payment does the bookkeeping for you. Our tool Tixward works this way:

  • You add your e-wallet or bank QR code, a card payment link or cash.
  • Buyers pick their tickets, pay you directly, and upload the screenshot and reference number on the same page.
  • You see each payment waiting for review, check it against your account, and approve it with one tap.
  • The buyer's QR tickets appear right away. Door staff scan them with any phone.
  • Unpaid reservations expire after the time you choose, and the seats go back on sale.

Tixward never touches the ticket money. Events with up to 300 paid tickets are free, and free tickets are always free. Bigger events pay a one-time fee based on size.

Avoiding common mix-ups

  • Two buyers, same amount, same time. This is why the reference number matters. Match each order by reference, not just by amount.
  • Paying for friends. If one person pays for several tickets, record every attendee's name, so the door list is correct.
  • Refunds. Decide your refund policy before you start selling, write it on the event page, and refund through the same method the buyer used.
  • Keep records. Download your list of orders and payments after the event, for your treasurer or for reporting to your school or church.

A quick setup checklist

  1. Choose two or three payment methods your buyers use.
  2. Write clear payment instructions, including the account name.
  3. Ask for a screenshot and reference number with every payment.
  4. Set a pay-by time and a refund policy.
  5. Check every payment in your own account before confirming.
  6. Send each buyer a ticket with a unique code.
