Reunions are mostly organized by volunteers with day jobs, using a group chat and good intentions. A simple timeline keeps the planning calm and makes sure everyone, including the classmates who are hardest to find, gets invited.

Four to six months before

Form a small committee

Three to six people is ideal. Give each person a clear role so nothing falls through the cracks:

  • Lead: keeps the timeline and makes final decisions.
  • Treasurer: handles the budget, payments and records.
  • Contacts: finds classmates and manages the invitation list.
  • Program: plans the activities, emcee and slideshow.
  • Venue and food: deals with the venue, caterer and suppliers.

Pick a date and a rough format

Run a quick poll in the batch group chat with two or three possible dates. Holiday seasons work well because many classmates come home to visit family, but venues book up early. Decide on the format: a dinner, a day out, a school visit plus party, or a whole homecoming weekend.

Start finding classmates

This always takes longer than expected. Start a shared list with names from the class list or yearbook, and add a column for how to contact each person. Ask everyone in the group chat to find two or three missing classmates each. Social media, mutual friends and former teachers are the best sources.

Three months before

Set the budget and ticket price

List the costs: venue, food, sound system, decorations, souvenirs, photographer, and a small buffer for surprises (about 10 percent). Divide by a realistic headcount, not the hoped-for one. For reunions, expect 40 to 60 percent of reachable classmates to attend.

Consider different ticket types: alumni, plus-ones, children, and maybe a "sponsor" ticket for classmates who want to give more. If you're inviting teachers, decide whether their tickets are free, and budget for them.

Book the venue and caterer

Ask about the minimum number of guests, the deadline for the final headcount, and the cancellation policy. Those dates become your registration deadlines.

Two months before: open registration

Announce the reunion with a clear post: date, time, venue, price, what's included, and how to register and pay. Then collect registrations in one place, not in scattered chat messages.

A reunion ticket page makes this much easier than chasing replies. With Tixward, for example, classmates pick their tickets, answer a question like their section or meal choice, and pay the treasurer's e-wallet or bank account directly. The treasurer reviews each payment and approves it, and classmates get a QR ticket. If someone reserves but doesn't pay within the time you set, their spot opens up again. Reunions with up to 300 paid tickets are free on Tixward.

Whichever tool you use, ask for the information you'll actually need later: full name and maiden name if different, section, meal choice or allergies, and whether they're bringing a plus-one.

One month before

  • Send reminders to classmates who haven't registered yet. A personal message from a close friend works better than another group announcement.
  • Plan the program. Keep speeches short. Popular parts are a slideshow of old photos, a "then and now" segment, a tribute to teachers and classmates who have passed away, and games that mix people from different groups.
  • Collect old photos for the slideshow. Ask classmates to send them to one email or folder, with names and years if possible.
  • Order souvenirs and name tags. Name tags with the name everyone used in school are a huge help after 10 or 20 years.

Two weeks before

  • Close registration, or set a final deadline that matches the venue's headcount date.
  • Give the final number and meal counts to the caterer.
  • Prepare the guest list, grouped by section if your batch is large. Print a sign-in sheet as a backup.
  • Assign volunteers for the registration table, program, photos and cleanup.

On the night

  • Set up a welcome table near the entrance with name tags, the guest list and someone friendly who remembers faces.
  • Check guests in quickly. Scanning QR tickets on a phone is faster than searching a printed list, and it records who arrived.
  • Have a plan for walk-ins. Someone always shows up without registering. Decide in advance whether they can pay at the door and how much.
  • Take a group photo early, before people start leaving.

After the reunion

  • Thank everyone in the group chat, and share the photos and a recap video. See how to make an event recap video.
  • The treasurer shares a simple report of money in and money out. Transparency keeps trust high for next time.
  • Download and keep your guest list and payment records before they're deleted from any tool you used.
  • Update the contact list. It's the most valuable thing you built, and the next reunion will start from it.
